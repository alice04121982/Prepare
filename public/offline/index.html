Stay Prepared

A practical, non-alarmist guide to what to keep on hand for social disruption — and realistically how long it lasts.

This page works without the internet. It is a single, self-contained file with no external scripts, fonts, or images — save it to your device, a USB drive, or print it, and it will still open and read correctly with no connection at all. Share it with anyone who might need it.

Why this exists

The UK government asks every household to be able to cope on its own for three days. In an emergency, help goes first to the people who need it most, and everyone else is expected to manage for a while. This guide is the practical version of that request: realistic scenarios, realistic durations, and a short list of things worth having at home to tide you over until the council, the utilities or the government can fix the problem or get help to you. Once your own household has what it needs, look around you. Neighbours who know each other get through a bad week faster.

Scenarios and realistic durations

Power outage

Typical duration: most resolve within hours; a small share last 1–3 days

Loss of mains electricity from a storm, grid fault, or planned load-shedding. What helps: torches and spare batteries, a way to charge a phone without mains power (a power bank or car charger), and food that needs no cooking.

Water supply disruption

Typical duration: usually under 48 hours

Mains water cut off or advised unsafe to drink, e.g. a burst main or a contamination notice. What helps: stored drinking water (2.5 to 3 litres per person per day, the current gov.uk figure), and a way to purify water if needed (boiling, or a filter/purification tablets).

Supply chain or delivery delays

Typical duration: days to a couple of weeks

Shops or deliveries disrupted by weather, fuel shortages, or logistics problems. What helps: a modest store-cupboard of shelf-stable food built up gradually over time — and importantly, not panic-buying, which is what actually causes shortages for everyone else.

Extreme weather event

Typical duration: hours to a few days, sometimes longer for widespread flooding or storms

Storms, flooding, heatwaves, or heavy snow that make it hard or unsafe to leave home or reach shops. What helps: enough food, water, and medication on hand for a few days, a battery or wind-up radio for updates, and warm layers or cooling measures depending on the event.

Local civil disruption

Typical duration: usually short-lived, localized, and resolves within days

Periods of local unrest, protest, or infrastructure strain that may briefly affect movement, shops, or transport in an area. What helps: the same core essentials below, staying informed through reliable local sources, and — as with every scenario here — checking in with neighbours rather than isolating.

Armed conflict

Typical duration: months or longer in affected regions, lived as repeated short disruptions — alerts lasting hours, power cuts of several hours a day, intermittent water and heating

War or sustained attacks on infrastructure in a modern, connected country, as Ukraine has experienced since 2022. For most people most of the time it looks like life continuing with recurring interruptions: scheduled power cuts, lost heating and water pumping, patchy mobile signal. What helps: a safe interior room with two walls between you and the outside, and knowledge of the nearest public shelter; a grab bag with documents, cash, medication, and chargers; power banks and, if affordable, a portable power station with a solar panel; extra stored water; a way to stay warm and cook in one room without mains power; a month of medication; an evacuation plan and a car kept half full; and official alert apps rather than rumour. What Ukraine showed above all is that neighbourhood and volunteer networks — delivering food to older people, sharing generators and internet, running warming points — were the response that worked.

Community and mutual aid

The single most effective form of resilience is a community that knows and looks out for each other — not a household going it alone. People consistently and reliably pull together during disruption: this is well documented across disasters of every kind. A few practical steps:

  • Know a few neighbours by name, especially anyone elderly, disabled, or otherwise more exposed to disruption.
  • Find or start a local mutual aid or neighbourhood network — many areas already have one from recent years.
  • Share skills: basic first aid, home repair, cooking for a crowd, childcare swaps. Skills spread further than stockpiles.
  • Agree a simple check-in habit with a few neighbours or family members for when something disrupts normal routine.

Myth-busting

Isn't this just "prepping" or doomsday thinking?

No. This is closer to what emergency services and governments already recommend: modest, realistic readiness for short, common disruptions — not preparing for civilisation's collapse.

Should I stockpile as much as possible?

No — large stockpiling is counterproductive. It empties shelves for everyone else, most disruptions are short, and unused stock often goes to waste. A modest, rotated supply covers the realistic cases above.

What's the single most useful thing to do first?

Build a 3-day supply of water, food, and any essential medication, and know two neighbours by name. Everything else here builds outward from that.
