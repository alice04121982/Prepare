# Stay Prepared: tone of voice

Drafted 18 September 2026 for Alice's approval. Nothing on the site has
been changed to match this yet.

## What the research found

**The UK government's own register (prepare.campaign.gov.uk, May 2024,
and the Resilience Action Plan, July 2025 and July 2026).** Plain, short,
second person, action first. "Taking action now will make it easier to
manage." It names hazards, not villains: flooding, power cuts, cyber
attacks, extreme weather, "a growing number of sub-threshold attacks from
Russia". It never says stockpile. Oliver Dowden's launch speech set the
line the whole campaign follows: "This is not about stockpiling, this is
about sensible safeguards" and "small things we can do today that might
make a big difference tomorrow". The 2026 campaign was briefed the same
way: "It won't be anything like duck and cover", "common sense, not
scaremongering".

**Sweden's "If Crisis or War Comes" booklet (MSB, 2018, reissued to every
household November 2024).** The best-written document in this field and
the model to steal from. It opens with a question, not a warning: "What
would you do if your everyday life was turned upside down?" Then a flat
list of what stops: the heating, the shops, the taps, cards and cash
machines, mobile networks, transport, medicines. It admits the feeling:
"Many people may feel a sense of anxiety when faced with an uncertain
world." And it sets the order of responsibility exactly as Alice wants
it: "In the event of a societal emergency, help will be provided first to
those who need it most. The majority must be prepared to cope on their
own for some time. The better prepared you are, the greater the
opportunity you will also have to help others." Household first, then
community, as a consequence rather than a slogan.

**The Guardian (Aaron Gell on left-leaning preppers).** Wry, humane,
sceptical of gear. "If you find yourself in a situation where you're
having to let off some rounds to protect your canned goods, you've already
failed." "Your ability to survive a disaster or thrive post-disaster is
contingent on really knowing your neighbors." Useful for the FAQ and
myth-busting voice, not for the home page.

**The news coverage.** Mainstream reports (ITV, LBC, Novara, IBTimes) all
reach for the word "stockpile" in the headline and "Russia" in the second
paragraph, then quote an official saying it is common sense. That gap
between headline and substance is the anxiety this site answers. The
Times and New York Times pieces were behind paywalls; the CNN piece on
Europe readying its citizens was blocked; their framing as reported
elsewhere matches the above.

## What was wrong with the current draft

The current home page leads with "The news is worse" and a sentence about
war, authoritarian politics, climate and inequality. That is editorial,
it is my voice rather than the government's or Sweden's, and it puts the
reader in a debate before it puts them in their kitchen. It also led with
community as "the most effective preparation", which inverts the order.
Sort your own household first. Community follows, and it follows better
when households are not the ones needing help.

## The voice

1. **Plain, short, second person.** Write like the gov.uk page, a degree
   warmer. Sentences under twenty words. No exclamation marks. No
   rhetorical flourishes.
2. **Open with a question the reader can answer.** "If the water went off
   tonight, how many bottles do you have?" Sweden and Dowden both do
   this. It moves people from the news to their cupboard in one line.
3. **Name the hazard once, plainly, then move on.** Power cuts, water
   notices, severe weather, cyber attacks on infrastructure, conflict in
   Europe. Not ideologies, not names of leaders, not "the rise of". The
   reader already knows what is on the news.
4. **Say why now in one honest paragraph and stop.** Every government in
   northern Europe has asked households to hold a few days of supplies
   since 2024. Report that. Do not add "they would not ask if they did not
   think it might be needed" or any line that turns the fact into a nudge.
   None of them say it is imminent, and neither do we.
5. **Household first, then everyone else.** Help goes to those who need it
   most, so the rest of us are expected to manage. The better prepared
   you are, the less you need and the more you can give. Community is the
   result of that, not the starting instruction.
6. **Sensible safeguards, never stockpiling.** Three days. Things you
   already buy. A few pounds a week. Never "hoard", "bunker", "bug out",
   "survive".
7. **Honest about the limit, briefly.** For the very worst cases there is
   little any household can do. Say it once, in the FAQ and once on the
   home page, and do not dwell.
8. **British and dry.** Understatement over emphasis. Humour only in the
   FAQ, and only at the expense of gear culture, never at the reader.
9. **No neat closers.** "The rest is a shopping list", "that is the whole
   job", "sorted": lines that tidy a paragraph away read as written by a
   machine and as smug. End on the instruction instead: be prepared, buy
   the essentials, store them away.
10. **Numbers are specific and sourced.** Litres, days, the gov.uk figure,
   the WHO figure. Never "plenty" or "as much as you can".

## Words

Use: keep, have, a few days, cope, manage, sensible, on hand, ready,
check, neighbour, household.

Avoid: stockpile, hoard, survive, survival, prepper, bunker, bug-out,
grab bag (say "a bag by the door"), doomsday, collapse, threat level,
fascism, authoritarian, annihilation, macho, "the news is worse".

## Sample copy for approval

**Home page hero**

Eyebrow: Simple steps for a difficult few days

Heading: If the power, water or shops stopped for three days, would you be
all right?

Lede: The government now asks every UK household to be able to cope on its
own for seventy-two hours. This site takes that advice and makes it
practical: what to keep,
how much, how long it lasts, and what to do first.

Buttons: Start with the checklist. Why three days?

**Second section: what might actually stop**

An emergency can mean the things we rely on stop working for a while.
Severe weather, a fault in the grid, a cyber attack on a water company, or
disruption from a conflict elsewhere in Europe can all have the same
effect at home:

- The heating goes off.
- The shops run short, or cannot take cards.
- Nothing comes out of the taps.
- Cash machines and card readers stop.
- Mobile networks and the internet go down.
- Buses and trains stop.
- Prescriptions are hard to get.

Most of these last hours or days, not weeks. All of them are easier with a
few things in the cupboard and a plan you made while everything worked.

**Third section: first you and yours, then everyone else**

In any emergency, help goes first to the people who need it most. Everyone
else is expected to manage for a while. So the order is simple. Sort your
own household out: water, food, light, warmth, medication, a way to hear
the news. Then look around you. The better prepared you are, the less you
need, and the more you can give to the neighbour who could not prepare.
Communities do pull together in a crisis. They pull together faster when
fewer households are in trouble.

**Fourth section: why now**

Since 2024 the UK, the EU, Sweden, Finland, Norway and France have all
asked their citizens to keep a few days of supplies at home. The reasons
they give are the same: severe weather, attacks on power and water
systems, cyber attacks, and the possibility of conflict in Europe. None of
them say an attack is imminent. All of them have decided it is no longer
sensible to assume it cannot happen. That is as far as the worry needs to
go. Be prepared: buy the essentials and store them away, so you have what
you need if the power goes off, the water stops or the shelves are empty.

**Closing line**

Three days of supplies and a plan on the fridge. That is the whole job.
